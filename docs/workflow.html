Turning a game idea into a real Roblox game

Every step below is one of two things: an AI making a judgement call, or a script doing the same thing every time. The whole design is about moving as much work as possible out of the first kind and into the second. AI steps cost real money and never give you the same answer twice. Scripts are free and always do.

AI step · costs money, never repeats exactly script · free, identical every run a file it produces

STEP 1

The interview

You get asked about your game idea. It looks up real competing games first, then questions you over several rounds, and writes down every answer next to who decided it: you, or it. That last part matters more than it sounds. Every later step reads it to know what it is allowed to change and what is yours and settled.

Why there are two checkers. The script goes first and catches everything you could count. That way the AI spends its money on judgement instead of on counting rows, which is the expensive way to do arithmetic.

STEP 2

The critics

Only read for judgement now: does this contradict that, would this really work. Everything countable is already gone.

The one rule that makes this affordable. A writer may not read another topic's work and may not search the web. It gets both handed over, already summarised. Reading everyone else's work used to cost roughly 86,000 tokens per document just to find out where your own job stopped, and it never actually prevented the overlap it was meant to prevent. One writer handling a whole topic cannot contradict itself.

STEP 3

The handoff

Deliberately a script and not an AI. A script cannot interpret, and that is exactly the point. It collects the numbers people wrote down, refuses the whole batch if two people claimed the same one, and checks them against rules like "the rarer plant has to be worth more" and "the area has to be physically big enough to hold that many plants".

Collect the numbers

Pulls the tagged numbers out of every decision document. If two documents claim the same one, it stops and names both files.

the build order

11 pieces of code to write, in the order they have to be written, each with a way to tell it works.

Why this exists at all. Someone built the game by hand once, first. Every number on that list of 11 is something they had to invent on the spot because no document gave them one: how many plants, how rare each kind is, what upgrades cost, how often the game saves, and the names of the six buried objects, which was squarely a designer's job. The list came from real gaps, not from theory.

STEP 4

The screens, then the game

This exists because Claude's Roblox screens were reliably ugly, and the cause turned out to be two missing pieces rather than badly written instructions. Nothing stopped it inventing its own colours, and it never once looked at what it had made. A fixed palette fixes the first. Drawing it and then criticising it fixes the second.

The known weak point. This can build exactly one shape of screen: a panel in the middle holding a grid of things. The design department keeps asking for other shapes, like a bar that stays on screen while you play, a settings list, a map, a text box. Everything feeding into it just got four times cheaper, so this is now the narrowest part of the whole pipeline.

Three rules everything else follows from

Breaking any one of these is how the whole thing quietly gets worse again.

Handoffs are code, never written instructions

Every point where one step feeds the next is a script, not a memo. Written instructions get re-read and re-interpreted by whoever picks them up, and that reinterpretation is exactly where two AIs quietly drift into building two different games.

Make the bad version impossible, rather than fixing it later

A fixed palette, an automatic checker, a rule that refuses. All better than making one screen look nicer by hand. The checker rejects two plant types that share a shape, so you can always tell them apart even in black and white. Nobody has to remember to check.

Limit the waffle, never the checklists

Learned the hard way this week. Given a flat limit on document length, a writer cut a twelve-item list of banned things down to six and threw away every specific example, while keeping all the paragraphs explaining its own reasoning.

Nobody can check "not spooky". Anybody can check "no cobwebs". Lists are now exempt from the limit.
